The guides here answer the questions that come before choosing a payoff order. Two of them deal with method: avalanche against snowball, which differ in total interest and in the odds you stay with the plan, and negotiating a lower rate, which changes the arithmetic more than any ordering can. Four deal with a specific kind of debt, credit cards, student loans, medical bills and consolidation, because the rules and the leverage differ in each. The last two deal with what makes a plan survive contact with real life: keeping an emergency fund while paying debt down, and the budget that funds the extra payment. Each guide states what it does not cover and where the figures come from. None of them recommends a lender, and none is paid for by one.

These guides are written for someone who already has a balance and wants to know what to do about it, not for someone comparing credit products. Each one covers a single situation, says which figures it relies on, and lists the official sources at the end so any number can be checked at its origin.

Read them in the order the problem presents itself. The comparison between the avalanche and snowball methods comes first, because the order in which debts are paid changes the total cost more than any other single decision. The guide matching your highest-rate debt comes next, since that is where the money is actually leaking. Budgeting and rate negotiation come afterwards: both are about finding the extra payment that makes a plan work, which is a different question from choosing the plan's shape.

Two subjects deliberately sit apart. The emergency fund guide argues for holding back money that could otherwise go to a balance, which looks like the wrong move until the first unexpected expense arrives and lands back on the card being paid down. The motivation guide deals with what happens in month four, when the novelty has worn off and the finish line is still far away, which is where most plans are abandoned rather than at the start.

Frequently asked questions

Which guide should I read first?
Start with the comparison of the avalanche and snowball methods, because the order in which you pay matters more than any other single decision. Then read the guide matching the debt that carries your highest rate. The budgeting and negotiation guides are worth reading once a plan exists, since both are about finding the extra payment that makes the plan work rather than choosing its shape.
Do these guides replace professional advice?
No. They explain how the arithmetic works and what the common options cost, which is enough for most situations. Once collections, wage garnishment, or a possible bankruptcy filing are involved, a non-profit credit counsellor accredited by the National Foundation for Credit Counseling will give better guidance than any calculator, usually at no charge for the first consultation.
Are the interest rates in these guides current?
The rate ranges quoted reflect typical offers at the time each page was last reviewed, and the date appears at the top and bottom of every guide. Rates on revolving credit move with the prime rate and can change between statements, so treat a range as an order of magnitude and check your own statement for the figure that applies to you.
Why do the guides recommend the avalanche method so often?
Because on arithmetic alone it always costs less: paying the highest rate first minimises the interest that accrues. The snowball method wins on a different measure, which is the probability that someone keeps going. Where the two differ by a few dollars, as they often do on smaller balances, the method you will actually stick to is the better one.
Can I use these guides outside the United States?
The arithmetic is universal, but the specifics are not. Credit scoring models, statutes of limitation on collections, and the tax treatment of forgiven debt all differ by country, and several guides reference federal programmes that exist only for US borrowers, such as income-driven repayment on student loans. Use the calculator freely; check the legal points against your own jurisdiction.
How often are these guides updated?
Each page carries the date of its last review at the top and in the footer, and the sources it relies on are listed at the end. Guides covering programmes that change with federal policy, student loan repayment in particular, are reviewed more often than those covering arithmetic, which does not change. A date more than a year old is a signal to verify against the source.

Official sources

Every rate range and rule on this page traces back to the publications below. No figure is taken from a third-party summary.

  • Consumer Financial Protection Bureau

    Rules on debt collection, validation notices and what a collector may and may not do.

  • Federal Reserve, G.19 Consumer Credit release

    Monthly figures on revolving and non-revolving consumer credit, and average interest rates.

  • Internal Revenue Service, Topic 431

    Tax treatment of cancelled or forgiven debt, which is generally taxable income.

  • Federal Student Aid, repayment plans

    Official terms of income-driven and standard repayment plans for federal student loans.
